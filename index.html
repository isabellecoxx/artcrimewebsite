Case Studies

Brussels, Belgium

Equestrian Statue of Leopold II

An equestrian statue of King Leopold II located in Brussels, Belgium. This monument has been the subject of controversy due to Leopold's role in the exploitation of the Congo Free State. Some view it as national Belgian pride, others see it as a symbol of colonial oppression.

Oświęcim, Poland

Auschwitz Concentration Camp

A former concentration camp where millions of people (primarily Jews) were imprisoned and killed during World War II. The camp has been preserved as a museum and memorial to the victims of the Holocaust, but it has also been criticized for its commercialization.

Washington, D.C.

Lincoln Emancipation Memorial

A monument dedicated to Abraham Lincoln located in Washington, D.C. It honors Lincoln's role in the emancipation of enslaved people in the United States. However, it has been heavily criticized for its portrayal of African Americans, particularly in the way it depicts their role in the abolition of slavery.

Our Conclusion

Across the three case studies, a clear pattern emerges in how monuments shift in meaning over time depending on who is interpreting them and what values are being applied. The Leopold II statue, the Emancipation Memorial, and the Auschwitz-Birkenau Memorial each show different relationships between history, power, and public memory. Some were built to celebrate figures now widely criticized, while others were created to remember suffering but still raise questions about representation. What connects them is that their meaning is not fixed. It changes with public awareness and with the communities most affected. Because of this, no single framework fully fits every case. Each monument requires a balance of historical context, intent, and real-world impact.

Deciding whether a monument should be removed or maintained depends largely on its impact in the present, not just its origin. In the Leopold II statue, the continued symbolism of colonial violence has made it a source of harm for many, which supports arguments for removal. The Auschwitz-Birkenau Memorial, however, shows how a site of trauma can function as education and remembrance without glorifying its history. The Emancipation Memorial sits in between, where its intended message is complicated by imagery that reinforces inequality. Across all three, vandalism appears as a form of response that can force public attention onto these tensions. While it is still debated, it can act as a way of challenging how history is displayed in public space.

Overall, this project shows that monuments are not just historical objects but active parts of ongoing public debate. They reflect how societies choose to remember the past and whose experiences are prioritized in that memory. The conflicts surrounding them reveal that history is not neutral in public spaces, but constantly shaped by present-day values and pressures. What stands out is that there is no simple answer to what should be done with controversial monuments. Instead, each case forces a decision about responsibility, memory, and impact.
