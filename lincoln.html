Lincoln Emancipation Memorial

About the Monument

The Lincoln Emancipation Memorial is a monument located in Lincoln Park, Washington D.C., intended to honor the President’s signing of the Emancipation Proclamation in 1863. The statue was designed by architect Thomas Ball, and it was placed in the Park in 1875, and dedicated April 14, 1876, on the eleventh anniversary of Lincoln’s death (Savage). This monument has faced controversy from the time it was completed, from notable Black speakers such as Frederick Douglass, to present day, resulting from the 2020 Black Lives Matter Movement’s increased focus on the memorialization of the United States’ past concerning racial injustices. However, some supporters argue that removing this monument would act as an erasure of history and could discount the original intent of the monument, and additionally, others see no issue with the monument’s existence at all. These opposing views lead to my guiding question for this case study: is destruction the solution to monuments that have remained controversial for centuries and do not reflect the beliefs of the people represented, and is there a middle ground (for example, preservation and recontextualization)?

Why It Matters

The controversy surrounding this statue can be traced back to its origins as a memorial proposed by formerly enslaved and newly emancipated African American citizens. The initial donation of five dollars towards the cause came from a woman by the name of Charlotte Scott upon the event of Lincoln’s death in 1865, as a means of paying tribute to the President who had secured freedom for millions of Black Americans like her (National Park Service). Ultimately, $17,000 was raised for the statue, which was an enormous amount in those days, especially donated by formerly enslaved individuals who had little to no income or ability to generate wealth for themselves. However, despite their significant contributions, none of the Black donors had a say in the design of the monument; this was left up to the all-white Western Sanitary Commission led by Reverend William Greenleaf Eliot of Missouri (Chappell). Eliot commissioned Thomas Ball to design the sculpture, depicting an enslaved man kneeling at the feet of President Lincoln, who extends his arm over the man in a gesture of gracious saviorism. The final statue, made of bronze and approximately nine feet tall without factoring in the towering granite pedestal it perches on, showcases a grand display of inferiority of African Americans, with the kneeling man in question wearing sparse clothing, bearing a closer resemblance to a subservient animal than a free human being (“Emancipation Group”). The identity of the man who the statue is modeled after is also a topic of great contention and represents the lack of a voice that Black citizens had in designing the monument.

The likeness of the kneeling figure is based on Archer Alexander, a formerly enslaved man who was the last person in the state of Missouri to be captured under the 1850 Fugitive Slave Act, a mandate that declared all escaped enslaved people were to be returned to their former captors (Savage). Alexander was sheltered by William Greenleaf Eliot while Missouri passed its own emancipation act, and afterwards, he worked as a servant for the Eliot family until his death in 1879, with Eliot posthumously publishing a book about his life in 1885 (Savage). Eliot was inspired by Alexander’s story, and suggested to the Western Sanitary Commission that his image be used in the statue without Alexander’s consent. Despite a lack of agreement on the part of their model, they went ahead with the design, and the problematic portrayal of Alexander has remained since. One issue with the statue is that, although Alexander was eventually emancipated, this was not due to the 1863 Emancipation Proclamation, because Missouri was a Union border state where this mandate did not apply. Thus, the statue is an inaccurate depiction of history, and credits Lincoln with Alexander’s freedom when, in fact, it did not come until over eight months after the original proclamation was made (Savage). Secondly, in Eliot’s book, he describes Alexander as, “happy, contented, and grateful,” and, in many ways, “only a grown-up child” (Savage). This greatly infantilizing and oversimplified description of Alexander’s character is on full display in this monument, which can be extended to act as evidence of greater societal attitudes towards Black Americans after the end of the Civil War. Therefore, the existence of this statue and its continued display in Lincoln Park could be seen as a complete distortion of history, and an affront to the image of African Americans who fought for their freedom instead of graciously and subserviently accepting it from their white liberators.

"Happy, contented, and grateful," and, in many ways, "only a grown-up child." - Eliot's description of Archer Alexander (Savage)

For these reasons, this statue has been criticized by many, including Frederick Douglass. In a speech upon its dedication in 1876, Douglass voiced his opinion about the monument, stating, “It must be admitted, truth compels me to admit, even here in the presence of the monument we have erected to his memory, Abraham Lincoln was not, in the fullest sense of the word, either our man or our model. In his interests, in his associations, in his habits of thought, and in his prejudices, he was a white man” (Douglass). He goes on to criticize Lincoln’s upholding of the Fugitive Slave Act, as well as a man that did indeed harbor prejudices towards African Americans, just like many of his white countrymen. The existence of this statue wrongly paints Lincoln as the “Great Emancipator,” but as Douglass points out, this is an inaccurate term given to the President in order to frame him as some sort of savior to Black Americans. One possible solution to this inaccurate portrayal would be to create a statue where, as Douglass calls for in a letter written a few weeks later, a Black citizen is “not couchant on his knees like a four-footed animal, but erect on his feet like a man” (Savage). As it stands today, the Lincoln Emancipation Memorial is a painful reminder of the United States’ history of prejudice against African Americans, as well as a willingness to perpetuate false historical narratives that paint oppressors in a better light than they earned in their lifetime.

"Abraham Lincoln was not, in the fullest sense of the word, either our man or our model. In his interests, in his associations, in his habits of thought, and in his prejudices, he was a white man." — Frederick Douglass (1876)

How, then, can we address such an issue when there are monuments across our country that send similar oppressive messages? Well, the city of Boston, Massachusetts had a solution in 2020. For almost 150 years, Boston’s Park Square housed a copy of the Emancipation Memorial, until it started to face vandalism and calls for removal as a result of the Black Lives Matter movement in the summer of 2020 (Chappell). It was taken down in December of that year after a petition demanding its removal reached 12,000 signatures, and according to the Mayor’s office, “The decision for removal acknowledged the statue’s role in perpetuating harmful prejudices and obscuring the role of Black Americans in shaping the nation’s freedoms” (Chappell). Plans were discussed for the display of the statue to exist in a different, more historical context, but six years later, it sits in storage. The case of the Boston statue acts as an imperfect model for how a city might address similar controversial monuments that perpetuate harmful messages, yet also hold strong historical significance. In this scenario, the vandalism that preceded the monument’s removal acted as a call for social change, so it could certainly be seen as effective. However, in this case, the monument’s destruction and lack of current visibility is a topic of debate, with some arguing that it being on view is essential to learning our country’s history, and others maintaining that the statue should be destroyed for good.

"The decision for removal acknowledged the statue's role in perpetuating harmful prejudices and obscuring the role of Black Americans in shaping the nation's freedoms." - Mayor's office (Chappell)

Points for Removal

  • People in favor of removing this monument believe that its continued display reinforces the subservience of African Americans to their white "liberators," especially in such a public location.
  • Critics of the statue also point to the fact that Archer Alexander, the real-life model for the statue, was not actually freed by the Emancipation Proclamation, so it presents a false and harmful account of American history.

Points for Restoration

  • Some believe that if we take down this monument, we refuse to acknowledge the racist ideologies that have plagued our country for centuries, so it must remain visible to learn from our past.
  • Others do not see an issue with the content of the statue, and think that Lincoln deserves to be honored and that the removal of this statue would be disrespectful to him.
  • Still others point to the fact that the proceeds for this statue came from emancipated slaves, and obscuring it from public view would put their efforts to waste.

Our Perspective

Our group’s perspective aligns closely with the Boston monument example, though we support following through on the display of the monument in a more historical and educational context. We do not believe that absolute destruction is the solution to monuments whose histories are more complex than existing simply to glorify one oppressive figure (such as the King Leopold II statue), but they should certainly not be displayed in a celebratory context without additional information provided about those who have been adversely impacted by the statue’s history. The Washington D.C. monument receives a lot of viewership, as it stands in our nation’s capitol and commemorates one of our most famous Presidents, which makes it a tourist attraction for many who may not be as familiar with American history. As such, leaving the monument unchanged is an irresponsible action that perpetuates harmful historical messages and whitewashes the legacy of imperfect historical figures. At the very least, a commemorative plaque is necessary to pay homage to Archer Alexander and the millions of other human beings who have been victimized by the institution of American slavery. Given that this D.C. monument has also been a recipient of vandalism in the wake of the Black Lives Matter movement.
