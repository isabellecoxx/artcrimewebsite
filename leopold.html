Equestrian Statue of Leopold II

About the Monument

The Equestrian Statue of King Leopold II is located in Brussels, Belgium. This monument stands in the center of the Place du Trône, located near the Royal Palace and governmental buildings, including the Belgian Federal Parliament. The statue was originally proposed as a way to honor and commemorate the king shortly after his passing. Particularly for his legacy of establishing a “great colony.” It was designed by the sculptor Thomas Vincotte, who had strong political ties to the late king and commissioned many projects under him. The monument was funded through public donations from royalty, aristocrats, and citizens alike. The government raised about 600,000 Belgian francs, which could be anywhere from five hundred thousand to a million dollars today. After a brief delay due to the first World War, it was completed in 1926, and remains in the square to this day.

Historical Context

To understand the controversies surrounding the monument, it's important to reflect on who it’s meant to represent.King Leopold II came to power in 1865. The Belgian monarchy is similar to Britain in that it’s a constitutional monarchy. Royals hold little political power, and act mainly as symbolic leaders. Scholars suspect that Leopold was unsatisfied with his reign of Belgium. Seeing the colonization of Africa by various European powers, he was interested in gaining a territory which he could rule over and extract resources from. He followed the stories of Henry Morton Stanley in particular, a Welsh-American explorer of Africa. His travels through Africa were published in newspapers, in which he described the Congo river as the “grand highway of commerce to west central Africa.” (Stanley).

The king invited Stanley to Belgium, and hired the explorer to work as his personal agent in Africa. While he never personally visited the Congo, he used Stanley as well as his lobbying powers to gain control over the region. Stanley would sign treaties with local Congolese chiefs. These were often incredibly exploitative, as chiefs would unknowingly sign away their rights to their land in exchange for materials. Lobbying the United States and various European powers, he posed his mission as a humanitarian effort, framing his pursuit of the Congo as an effort to end the slave trade as well as make scientific advancements. He went so far as to establish the International African Association, holding a conference in Brussels to discuss these goals. Eventually, during the Berlin Conference in 1884, he was granted possession of the Congo River Basin.

In 1885, Leopold was declared as the ultimate authority over Congo, which he named the Congo Free State. The region was rich with ivory and rubber, and he used the labour of natives to exploit such resources. He enforced strict rules so the Congolese people could only trade with his agents, and they were often poorly compensated for their efforts. When workers inevitably refused to continue under the harsh conditions, he used cruel tactics to ensure his control. He bought slaves and utilized their labour, as well as taxing the chiefs he made deals with to supply workers to collect resources. He enforced strict quotas, and often used violence to enforce his strict expectations, keeping families as ransom and threatening the workers at gunpoint. Any effort of rebellion was stopped by his private army, the Force Publique, which slaughtered entire villages, cut off the limbs of innocents, and killed hiding natives in the forests. Eventually his crimes were discovered by shipping clerks and missionaries, and as his human rights violations became public knowledge, he was forced to give up possession of his colony to the Belgian government. Eventually, in 1960, Congo gained its independence and became the Democratic Republic of Congo of today.

Why It Matters

The call for removal of the statue of Leopold in Brussels became a major political movement in 2020, close to the 60th anniversary of Congo’s independence and during the international Black Lives Matter protests. The monument was seen as a symbol of colonial oppression and white supremacy, and was subject to vandalization and various protests. Tracy Bibo-Tanasia, a Belgian citizen from Congolese descent, openly discussed Belgium’s history of colonial oppression, and argued that reparations should be made and symbols of colonization removed.

“We must acknowledge that colonization, such as slavery, were racist systems that benefited one group and marginalized others. But the most important thing is recognizing that until today it has had an impact on marginalized groups,” she states in her open letter calling for reparations (Bibo-Tansia 4).

“We must acknowledge that colonization, such as slavery, were racist systems that benefited one group and marginalized others. But the most important thing is recognizing that until today it has had an impact on marginalized groups,” she states in her open letter calling for reparations (Bibo-Tansia 4). An activist group Reparons created a petition demanding the removal of the Equestrian statue along with many others, and the movement gained over 80,000 signatures and 10,000 protestors. Red paint was splashed on the statue, symbolizing the bloodshed the monarch was responsible for. Anti-racist sentiments were written as graffiti numerous times. These acts were all in an effort to pressure the government into taking action to remove these statues and provide reparations.

Despite the outrage, there were some who criticized the movement. Some saw the monument not as a symbol of colonial oppression, but of Belgium pride and honoring the monarchy. Those against the removal of the statue often cited nationalism as their reasoning. A letter defending the monument was sent to the mayor of Brussels, and a counter petition was created gaining 20,000 signatures. The arguments for the restoration of the monument did little to stop the outrage, but it did demonstrate the nationalistic side of Belgium.

In response to the public discourse, on the 60th anniversary of Congo’s independence, King Philipe wrote a letter to Félix Antoine Tshisekedi Tshilombo, the president of the Democratic Republic of Congo. While he didn’t issue a full apology and wasn’t able to visit Congo personally due to the pandemic, he expressed his regrets for the past.

“I wish to express my deepest regrets for these wounds of the past,” he stated (Philippe).

This gesture had mixed reception from the Congolese. Some saw it as a foundational step in the right direction, others found it lacking and wanted more concrete reparations. Some efforts have been made since by the Belgian government to improve its relations. Five Metis (mixed-race) women with a Congolese and Belgian parent sued the government for the emotional damages of being taken from their families to Belgium. They were awarded about 50,000 euros each. Additionally, the Working Group on Colonial Symbols in Public Spaces was formed, and activists pushed for reparations and outlined potential actions the government could take. There’s still much progress to be made and a lack of true accountability from the government, but ultimately there seems to have been a step in the right direction.

Points for Removal

  • The activist group Reparons petitioned for the removal of all statues of King Leopold in Brussels which garnered over 80,000 signatures.
  • Belgian citizens with Congolese origins, like Tracy Bibo-Tansia, found the statue a disturbing reminder of the oppression of the Congolese people.
  • The push for removal was part of a broader anti-racist movement in 2020 inspired by the murder of George Floyd and heightened by the murder of a teenage Moroccan killed during a police chase in Brussels.

Points for Restoration

  • A counter petition was formed that garnered 20,000 signatures.
  • Some accused the push for removal to be driven by "globalist" or political agendas rather than balanced historical understanding.
  • Some believe removing statues unfairly attacks the legacy of the Belgian monarchy and national history.

Our Perspective

After many thoughtful discussions as a group, we came to the conclusion that the statue of Leopold should be removed from public view and destroyed. The monument has always stood as a symbol of a colonial dictator who was responsible for genocide. We believe it’s most important to prioritize the perspectives of the oppressed, and from Congolese and Belgian citizens with Congolese origins, the monument is widely viewed as a symbol of oppression. While the possibility of recontextualization came up, we agreed that the best course of action would ultimately be destruction. There are pictures and accounts of Leopold’s life, and a statue that was built in his honor is not required to have a historical understanding of the monarch.
