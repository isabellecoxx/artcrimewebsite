Auschwitz Concentration Camp Memorial

About the Monument

The nazi concentration camps were detention centers established from 1933 to 1945, which were initially designed to incarcerate political prisoners and communists. They expanded to hold not only Jewish people but also Jehovah's Witnesses, homosexuals, and prisoners of war. While many began as labor camps, the system evolved to become industrial-scale extermination camps designed for killing millions in gas chambers. Chełmno was the first extermination camp to be established, starting operations in December 1941. When the Nazis saw that they got away with the sickening actions taking place at Chelmno, then Auschwitz-Birkenau was established in March 1942 as the largest of the extermination camps. There are a few of these camps still up for people to visit throughout Europe, with us focusing specifically on Auschwitz-Birkenau since it is the most visited of all of the camp memorials. The question I want to tackle here is whether it is beneficial enough in terms of the memory of the victims, the events, and the lessons learned from them, to keep up landmarks for such a horrible tragedy, or would it be better to get rid of these horrifying landmarks for the sake of preserving the dignity of the victims and not let them be subject to disrespect?

Why It Matters

Starting off with reasons why we should preserve the landmark, Many of the visitors are descendants of survivors or victims seeking to see what their family went through and to connect further with their family history. This is discussed by Hodgkinson, who argues that memorialisation, both in terms of personal remembrance of ancestors who lost their lives in these places, is a key driver (Hodgkinson). Also, by visiting, descendants are getting a grasp of the suffering of their ancestors. This act of remembrance serves as a refusal to let the victim be forgotten, effectively pushing back against the Nazi’s goal of erasure. When you read that "1.1 million people died at Auschwitz-Birkenau," the number is so large it becomes abstract since the human brain will naturally struggle to visualize a million of anything. Due to this, some people visit to see the true scale of what happened rather than hearing stories about the events. Seeing the physical evidence like piles of shoes, suitcases, or hair transforms statistics into the reality of individual human lives (Historical Collection / Museum / Auschwitz-Birkenau). These physical objects trigger a specific type of empathy because we use these same items every day. Since we all wear shoes, use combs, and carry keys. Seeing a rusted pair of glasses or a cracked brush gives one the thought of "That could be me" or "That looks like my mother’s brush". Also, seeing a physical object that was once held by a victim gives it more weight since seeing the object and its condition makes the history feel present rather than distant.

One of the scariest realizations that I have realized when researching this topic is that the Holocaust didn't begin with gas chambers and concentration camps, it began as a slow buildup of hate with words, and later became paperwork, then kept evolving until it became what it was. These murders required the participation of architects, train conductors, clerks, and etc., proving that genocide is very much a logistical process that needs plenty of people on board as much as it is a hateful one. Visiting landmarks like this with a dark history can instill a sense of moral responsibility and help recognize the early warning signs for events like this. This moral responsibility is talked about here in a journal by Kraig, with how the eyewitnesses and survivors of the holocaust’s physical presence provided an anchor of truth that made denial or distortion nearly impossible in their company. Now that they are diminishing, our generation needs to not forget these events just because it feels like a long time ago, and to uphold that responsibility to prevent events like this from taking place again (Kriag 1).

"The eyewitnesses and survivors of the holocaust's physical presence provided an anchor of truth that made denial or distortion nearly impossible in their company." (Kraig 1)

In today’s world of misinformation and skepticism, denial of the Holocaust even happening is becoming more common than ever. Keeping up the landmark along with its physical ruins and archives at these sites serves as irrefutable proof that the holocaust was something that took place in history. This issue is also addressed by the Auschwitz-Birkenau Memorial and Museum itself, with them talking about how they maintain these ruins specifically to prevent the "falsification of history" (www.auschwitz.org). In an era where online misinformation can skew our perception of the past, the physical evidence of Auschwitz acts as a material anchor. While digital narratives can be edited or deleted, the worn-down bricks of the barracks remain undeniable.

It also shows that ordinary people could participate in or remain indifferent to such horrors, and it is something we have to look out for and actively go against as a society when something like this shows itself. This is talked about in the book “Becoming Evil: How Ordinary People Commit Genocide and Mass Killing” by James Waller where he gives a story about Franz Ziereis, who was the commandant of the Mauthausen concentration camp from 1939 to 1945 and how he was described by those who knew him as a "model husband" and "devoted father" who looked like "someone’s father on a weekend stroll" (Waller 5) These behaviors don’t originate from people just being evil from birth but flaws within societies or within ourselves that we need to be aware of. By visiting the site, we are forced to confront what being evil means or how one becomes evil, such as the idea that under the right social pressures and ideologies, the "ordinary" person is capable of committing unspeakable horrors.

While these reasons for preservation are very strong, there is also the other side of the spectrum, which says that we should take down these landmarks for various reasons that we will talk about here. Firstly, some people disrespect the landmark for various reasons, such as being holocaust deniers or neo-nazis or whatever the case may be. This leads to further reason to take the place down, as Boyes talks about in a 2009 newspaper article where the “Arbeit Macht Frei” sign was stolen. This sign holds heavy historical value as a symbol of the lives lost at Auschwitz-Birkenau. He commented that this incident was an attempt by right-wing extremists to muddy the narrative of the Holocaust or prove the genocide was exaggerated. When a site of such atrocities becomes a target for hate speech or vandalism, it raises the question of whether we are inadvertently providing a stage for those who wish to celebrate the atrocities. These type of incidents and disrespect that come from them feels that the memory of these people is constantly being disrespected and that rather than have a memorial and museum of the events that happened, we should instead remove the landmark since it would be more dignified and allow these individuals to rest without being subjected to further public dishonor.

Some also argue that the modern-day Auschwitz-Birkenau Memorial and Museum has become very commercialized and digitized to the point where it can feel like the weight of the actual event is being downplayed. This is seen with how they have digitized Auschwitz-Birkenau with having 360-degree panoramic virtual tours online and strong social media presences for the sake of promotion and further showcases (Dalziel 3). This dilutes the weight of the effects that took place there and makes it more of a “cool tourist site to see” rather than a memorial in memory of the people who passed there, which feels very much disrespectful to the history and the memory of the people and their families. The term "dark tourism" is often used to describe this phenomenon, which is visiting sites associated with death, suffering, or disaster for the sake of education, remembrance, curiosity, but can easily trickle into the commodification of suffering and disrespectful tourist behavior.

To further expand on the point of diluting the weight of events that took place, Marc Horne, a journalist from The Times, talks about how there has also been a recent push from delegates and representatives from the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum in Washington, who are now giving lectures on how to prevent people from taking "selfies at sites of violent death" (Horne). The phenomenon of tourists smiling for photographs in front of gas chambers or balancing on the railway tracks shows a huge disconnect from the site's history. This shows how, as time has passed by, people have come to feel desensitized by these events and therefore just treat them like tourist attractions rather than places of mourning and reflection. The historical weight of the events is lost, resulting in a deep lack of respect for the victims and their descendants

"Selfies at sites of violent death" - lectures now given on preventing disrespectful tourist behavior at Auschwitz-Birkenau. (Horne)

Points for Removal

  • When millions of people visit these sites to take selfies or follow a tourist itinerary, the gravity of the mass murder is diluted or even lost.
  • Charging for entry or selling souvenirs nearby turns a site of genocide into a commodity, which is inherently disrespectful to the victims.
  • For some visitors, especially those with a personal family history or existing psychological vulnerabilities, visiting these sites can lead to severe anxiety, depression, or PTSD.

Points for Restoration

  • Many of the visitors are descendants of survivors or victims seeking to see what their family went through and to connect further with their family history.
  • Some people visit to see the true scale of what happened rather than hearing stories about the events. Seeing the physical evidence like piles of shoes, suitcases, or hair transforms statistics into the reality of individual human lives.
  • Visiting landmarks like this with a dark history can instill a sense of moral responsibility and help recognize the early warning signs for events like this.

Our Perspective

We are of the opinion of the first half of this paper, we should preserve Auschwitz-Birkenau because of the heavily impactful history that lies there, as well as the lessons that are learned here with the realization of the impact that humans can have on this world when extremist ideologies are let loose, and if collective silence follows these types of events. Also, to learn these lessons and take away that we should never turn away and let something like this happen again. My groupmates had similar viewpoints with Rowan bringing up how the Nazi concentration camps should be preserved to the best of our ability because destroying them would erase the memory of the millions of people who lost their lives there, and they act as a reminder of what can happen if the world decides to look the other way for too long. She also talks about how they are also incredibly emotionally significant places for victims’ families to visit and reconnect with their heritage and pay homage to their loved ones. Isabelle also brings up something similar when talking about how she believes the concentration camps should be preserved for remembrance of the victims who lost their lives there and how these are important sites for mourning those lost and remembering history. Removing these sites would be disrespectful to that history.

We believe that the discomfort we feel when visiting Auschwitz is precisely why it must remain. The preservation or dismantling of sites like Auschwitz-Birkenau poses the difficult debate of whether humanity should uphold the truth of the Holocaust and the dignity of the victims, or whether the risks of desecration of such a site are acceptable. As proof of the Holocaust that cannot be replaced in the current age of historical revisionism, these sites serve as a physical reflection of the atrocities of the Holocaust that can be felt and seen by those who visit. By preserving these ruins, humanity ensures that the horror of the Holocaust will always remain undeniable to the new generations of humanity. We live in an age where the last survivors are passing away, and soon, only the history books will remain to tell their story. I believe that the preservation of these sites should not be seen as an attempt to fixate on the tragedies of the past, but as a commitment to the future to never let something like this happen again. The stones of Auschwitz do not just belong to the past, they are a warning for every generation that follows.
